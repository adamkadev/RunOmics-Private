# Coaching methodology

Why the rules in `rules.md` say what they say, and how much each one is worth.

Every rule carries one of four labels. They are not decoration - most of what
running coaching repeats confidently is convention, and this file says so rather
than laundering it into evidence:

- **evidenced** - supported by controlled or large-cohort data
- consensus - respected practitioners agree, without a trial behind it
- _convention_ - widely repeated, no evidence located; often a round number
- **contested** - sources genuinely disagree, or the evidence is disputed

This file is the only normative source of reasoning here. A vendored general
coaching text (ColinEberhardt/claude-running-coach, MIT) sat alongside it for a
while; both of its load-bearing findings - 15-30% deloads, and the phase
structure of a 10-12 week 5K/10K plan - are recorded below, and it was deleted
rather than left as an uncited second opinion.

Numbers naming specific kilometres or paces were computed for one athlete at one
reference date and are illustrative. The live values come from the coaching
context; the reasoning is what transfers.

## Core principles

**Anchor on maxima the athlete has actually produced, never on a mean over a window that contains an absence.**

A rolling mean cannot distinguish 'did not run' from 'cannot run'. The 23.94 km 28-day figure is a travel artefact; the 45.0 km he completed last week and the 57.35 km best 4-week average are the facts about capacity. Anchoring on the mean is exactly what produced the 22 km prescription.

**Weekly volume and single-session distance recover on different clocks: return volume promptly, rebuild the long run against its own recent maximum.**

Reduced training is not cessation, so aerobic fitness and the 4:23/km threshold are essentially intact and weekly km can come back fast. Structural tolerance for one long effort is the slower variable, and it is the one quantity in this whole dossier with a hazard ratio attached to it. Put the caution on the session, not on the week.

**Hold prescribed paces fixed for the whole block and progress by volume, repetitions and rep length, in that order.**

Pace is the control variable. If pace and volume both move, neither the athlete nor the validator can attribute a bad week to anything, and the documented failure mode of controlled-intensity training is silent upward pace drift that turns a sub-threshold block into three threshold sessions a week.

**Constrain only quantities measured in kilometres that the validator can check. Display ACWR, TSB and load status; never let them gate a plan.**

ACWR is mathematically coupled, its chronic denominator is close to inert, and this athlete's denominator is travel-deflated so every normal week reads elevated. A metric with no demonstrated causal handle must not hold a veto over guardrails built on his own history.

**Carry the evidence label honestly: almost every number here is convention, and no training plan has been shown to prevent injury.**

The only two randomised tests of graded progression in runners were null, and a systematic review found very limited evidence linking load change to injury at all. The app may claim a block is well-organised and inside what the athlete has held. It may not claim the guardrails make him safe.

## Anchoring the block

- [_convention_] Set baselineKm = max(recent7DaysKm, recentWeeklyAverageKm) = max(45.0, 23.94) = 45.0 km, and capacityKm = max(best4WeekAverageKm, baselineKm) = 57.35 km. Write the 23.94 km 28-day mean to the plan as context annotated 'depressed by documented travel', and give it zero weight in every calculation.
  - This is what volumeBand() in src/application/coaching/volume.ts already computes; verified by reading the file. No trial prescribes the max() form - it is a reconciliation of 'recently carried load is informative' with 'a mean over an absence is not'.
- [consensus] Gate any return-to-training reduction on daysSinceLastRun and longestRecentGapDays, never on a depressed mean. Capture interruption cause as an explicit enum (travel | illness | injury | fatigue | none) rather than inferring it from volume, and branch: travel = no reduction; illness = clinician-guided graduated return, app emits no percentages; injury = clinician-set restart, not app-generated.
  - Every published return-to-run protocol, including Daniels', scales the restart to the LENGTH OF TIME OFF, so none applies to a runner whose last week was 45 km. The specific 33/50/67/100 percentages circulating for Daniels' table were quoted inconsistently across this dossier (67% vs 75%) from secondary sources and should not be hard-coded. trainingHistory.ts already carries the comment that travel and injury produce the same depressed mean and call for opposite prescriptions.
- [_convention_] Require the ceiling anchor to be recent: if best4WeekAverageKm is older than about 6 months, degrade the ceiling toward max(recent7DaysKm, best 4-week average within the window) rather than using the historical figure.
  - No source. Pure system hygiene: 57.35 km from eighteen months ago is history, not capacity, and the current code applies no staleness test. For this athlete the June block (48/45/48/54/55, all on 5 runs) is three months old, so the anchor is fresh and this rule does not bind.
- [_convention_] Record the provenance of the 4:23/km threshold (lab / field test / race-derived / Garmin auto-detect) and treat a Garmin-derived value as provisional until confirmed by a race or a 30-minute field test. Report the derived VDOT as a whole number with an explicit tolerance, not to one decimal.
  - Garmin/Firstbeat LT pace estimates validate poorly against laboratory constructs and Garmin returns no estimate on a substantial fraction of tests. A plus or minus 5 s/km error on the input moves VDOT by about 1.1 points, which is larger than the per-block update cap this rule set imposes. The commonly quoted cross-check (T is 25-30 s/mile slower than 5K, 10-15 s/mile slower than 10K) is Daniels' own verbal summary of the same equations that generate the table, so it is a regression test, not independent corroboration.

## Weekly volume and progression

- [_convention_] Keep the existing floor (0.95 x baselineKm, i.e. 42.75 km for every full week of this block) and ceiling (1.05 x capacityKm + 3 km = 63.2 km), and in this first post-interruption block keep the peak week at or below best4WeekAverageKm (57.35 km).
  - Both constants are the app's existing guardrails (FLOOR_RATIO 0.95, CEILING_RATIO 1.05, CEILING_ALLOWANCE_KM 3 in volume.ts). No trial supports any specific ceiling; the argument for staying under the demonstrated 4-week average is that it is re-entry into known territory rather than extrapolation.
- [**contested**] Limit any week-on-week step to the smaller of (running block peak x 1.12 + 2 km) and 8 km absolute, and treat any increase above 30% over a two-week window as out of bounds regardless.
  - Nielsen et al., JOSPT 2014 (874 novice runners) found elevated distance-related injury rates above 30% progression over a TWO-WEEK period versus under 10% - not >30% week-on-week, as this dossier repeatedly rendered it. Damsted et al., JOSPT 2019 (ProjectRun21, 261 half-marathoners; Damsted is first author, not Nielsen) found a 22.6% risk difference (95% CI 0.9-44.3, p=.041) at day 21 for 20-60% increases, not significant at days 56 or 98. Both cohorts were novice or first-time-distance runners. Daniels' independent step rule (add no more km per week than weekly session count, ~8 km at 5 runs) converges on the same place.
- [consensus] State in the plan's own copy that the ramp cap keeps the step inside what the athlete has recently held, and never that it prevents injury.
  - Buist et al., Am J Sports Med 2008: 532 novice runners randomised to a 13-week programme built on the 10% rule versus a standard 8-week programme, injury incidence 20.8% vs 20.3%. Bredeweg GRONORUN 2 (n=432, preconditioning) also null. Damsted et al. 2018 systematic review found only four eligible studies and 'very limited evidence'. Both RCTs used NOVICE runners, so they constrain what the app may CLAIM more than what it should DO.
- [_convention_] Add weekly volume by lengthening two or three easy runs by 1-2 km each, never by loading the whole increment onto the long run.
  - An inference from the session-spike finding below, not a tested distribution strategy: no study compares distribution tactics. The reasoning is that spreading an increment keeps every session inside the athlete's own 30-day session history.
- [_convention_] Never let one week carry both the block's largest weekly ramp and its largest single-session increase; if a week takes the biggest volume step, hold the long run flat that week.
  - Derived constraint with no source. Its real value is attribution: if week 3 goes badly, a single-variable week tells you which lever caused it.

## The long run and single-session load

- [_convention_] Add longestRunLast30DaysKm to trainingHistory() (max distanceKm over the trailing window) and flag any prescribed session that exceeds 1.10 x that value. State which window is used and make it a true 30 days, or rename the field to match the 28-day window trainingHistory already computes. Warn rather than hard-reject.
  - The underlying association is the strongest single running dataset available: Garmin-RUNSAFE, Br J Sports Med 2025, 5,205 runners and 588,071 sessions, HRR 1.64 (1.31-2.05) for sessions 10-30% above the 30-day longest run, 1.52 (1.16-2.00) for 30-100%, 2.28 (1.50-3.48) for >100%. But it is one unreplicated prospective observational cohort in self-selected Garmin users with a SELF-REPORTED injury outcome, the dose-response is non-monotonic, and reverse causation (a runner nursing a niggle runs short, then the session on which it crosses the reporting threshold is recorded as the culprit) cannot be excluded. The 10% figure is the upper edge of the study's reference category, not a demonstrated inflection point, so the cap derived from it is convention. Verified: no such field exists in src/domain/training/trainingHistory.ts today, and validateDraftVolume() in volume.ts only sums weekly km.
- [_convention_] Specify the field's edge cases before shipping it: if no run falls in the window, fall back to the longest run in the last 90 days and mark the plan for review; count races toward the maximum; recompute it at block generation only, not mid-week.
  - System-design necessity. The rule is not implementable without these, and the null case is precisely the one a returning athlete presents.
- [_convention_] Cap the long run at 30% of that week's planned volume as a secondary sanity bound. Where the 30-day rule and the 30% rule disagree, the lower binds.
  - Widely shared across Daniels, the RRCA and Hansons at 20-30%; no primary study establishes a percentage-of-week threshold. It exists to stop a compliant weekly total hiding one huge run plus filler.
- [_convention_] In a cutback week, trim the long run by only about 10-20% and take the bulk of the reduction from the midweek runs.
  - No source. The argument is mechanical rather than physiological: slashing the long run lowers the 30-day reference, so the first long run of the NEXT regenerated block registers as a spike and must itself be cut. Note this is optimising a guardrail's input, so it should not override a genuine need for recovery.
- [_convention_] Keep the long run entirely at easy pace during a 10K block - no progression, no fast finish, no embedded quality.
  - Standard 10K periodisation. With five running days and Saturday/Sunday adjacent, a hard Sunday makes three hard days in five and compromises Tuesday's threshold session. Pfitzinger's and Hudson's progression long runs are built for athletes with two to four more recovery days per week.

## Intensity, pace and quality dose

- [consensus] Hold every prescribed pace constant for all four weeks: threshold at the measured 263 s/km (tolerance +/- 3 s/km), intervals at roughly 241 s/km (+/- 4 s/km), easy in a wide band. Change pace only at block regeneration, by at most one VDOT point (about 5 s/km), and only on a race or completed benchmark - never on a Garmin VO2max change and never on how a session felt.
  - Daniels' own guidance is to hold a VDOT-derived pace through 4-6 weeks of consistent training before raising it one point. Do not cite Canova for this - his published method progressively moves paces toward race pace across a block, which is the opposite. No trial isolates the ordering.
- [consensus] Do not derate the threshold pace for the travel month. Prescribe 263 s/km from week 1 and treat week 1's session as a measurement: if reps at that pace feel materially harder than expected, re-test before week 2 rather than assuming.
  - Detraining figures (4-14% VO2max in short-term, i.e. under 4 weeks; 6-20% in long-term detraining) all come from complete CESSATION, and Mujika & Padilla's short-term paper is the relevant half. Reduced-volume training preserves or improves performance (Bosquet's taper meta-analysis: 41-60% volume cuts over 8-14 days with intensity AND FREQUENCY maintained). But the honest caveat is that the three weeks before last averaged about 17 km/week - deeper than the 41-60% band and longer than the 8-14 days that literature covers - and frequency during the travel month is unknown. So this is an extrapolation past the taper literature, not an application of it.
- [_convention_] Run exactly two quality sessions per week, on Tuesday and Thursday, in all four weeks including the cutback. Never three while the athlete runs five days.
  - Justify this on this athlete's arithmetic, not on the HIIT-frequency literature: with fixed Monday and Friday rest, a third quality day forces either back-to-back hard days or a hard day abutting the long run. Lenk 2025 (~9 per arm, exploratory) concluded 2-3 sessions may be effective and cannot establish that a third adds nothing.
- [_convention_] Cap threshold volume at 10% and interval volume at 8% of that week's PLANNED distance, computing both from the current week and never from best4WeekAverageKm. Warm-up, cool-down and recovery jogs sit outside the caps but count toward weekly volume, as do strides of about 20 seconds - a stride is neuromuscular, not a quality dose, and validateDraftIntensity reclassifies anything over 60 seconds wearing the label as an effort. A race is left out of the caps entirely: it is the point of the block, not a dose to ration against it.
  - Daniels' rules of thumb (T <=10%, I <=8% or 10 km, R <=5%), read from secondary summaries rather than the book, and stated there as per-WORKOUT limits. Re-keying the denominator to a historical average would make the cap stop binding in the cutback week, which is the one week it matters most. Note the cap genuinely binds at ~50 km/week: it pins the threshold session near 5 km, which is why progression must come from time at pace and rep structure.
- [_convention_] Make time-at-threshold strictly increase across the loading weeks, with a per-week ceiling of 25% and a two-week compounding ceiling of about 40%. A block whose threshold volume does not increase is not a training block. Reduced weeks are exempt, since shedding quality is what they are for.
  - **These ceilings almost never bind, because the 10% cap binds first.** With pace held fixed, time at threshold and distance at threshold are the same quantity, so capping the distance caps the time: at 48 km the session may be 4.8 km, and reaching 6.0 km needs a 60 km week. Progression by repetition count is therefore only available when weekly volume is itself climbing - 3 x 1600 m to 4 x 1600 m is a 33% step that needs the week to go from 48 to 64 km. At this athlete's volume the block progresses by lengthening three repetitions, roughly in step with the week. The worked block in SKILL.md predates this and does not satisfy the cap: 3 x 1600 m in 46.5 km is 10.3%, 4 x 1600 m in 50.5 km is 12.7%, 3 x 2000 m in 52.4 km is 11.5%.
  - Progressive overload applied to the block's primary quality session. The 25% band is convention with nothing measured behind it; the two-week compounding ceiling exists because Nielsen's threshold is defined over a two-week window. There is no lower bound - a 5% weekly step is legitimate when the per-session cap binds.
- [_convention_] Prescribe the recovery step inside a repeat by time, not by distance, with an easy pace range attached. Warm-ups, cool-downs and standalone easy runs stay on distanceMeters.
  - A rest interval is a dose of recovery time; distance-capping it inverts the control, handing more rest to the athlete jogging slower because they are tired or ill and less to the one jogging faster. Observed here on 2026-09-23: 600 m recoveries prescribed at 4:00 ran 3:59 / 4:28 / 4:42 while the athlete was ill, stretching the session 70 s past its structure, and the fixed distance made rep-to-rep comparison depend on how the jog went. Nothing is lost on the accounting side, because weekKm and validateDraftIntensity both convert a timed segment through its own prescribed pace. Strides recoveries were already timed; threshold was the inconsistency.

- [consensus] Keep at least 80% of weekly running easy, counted by distance and never by session count (two quality sessions out of five is 40% by session and is not a violation). No separate check enforces this: the 10% and 8% caps above bound hard running at 18% of the week, so a 20% ceiling behind them can never fire. Do not ask the generator to state its own accounting basis either - that is the assertable-rather-than-earnable mistake again; validateDraftIntensity counts by distance, converting a timed segment through its own prescribed pace, exactly as weekKm does.
  - Seiler's 80/20 is descriptive of elite endurance athletes' habits at 10-14 sessions a week, not a demonstrated optimum for a recreational runner, and his own session-goal and time-in-zone numbers differ (roughly 75-80% vs 91% low-intensity in the same dataset). The one randomised comparison in recreational runners (Munoz 2014, n=30, load-matched) favoured polarized on 10K time, but that is a post-hoc compliant-subgroup comparison; Festa 2019 (n=38) found a 40/50/10 distribution matched a polarized arm while saving 17% of training time. Treat 80% as a conservative ceiling on hard volume, not as a law.
- [_convention_] Name every intensity by its pace and its physiological name, never by a zone number. If a zone number must appear, state the model and basis ('Garmin 5-zone, %LTHR basis').
  - Seiler's three-zone Z2 is the between-thresholds band that polarized training avoids; Garmin's five-zone Z2 is easy running. Mixing the vocabularies silently changes the prescription.
- [consensus] Do not program double-threshold days or a third sub-threshold session. Borrow only the Norwegian intensity-control principle: set rep pace at the fastest speed repeatable in 48 hours and finish each session with at least one rep in reserve. Label the block 'controlled threshold', not 'Norwegian method'.
  - The double day is a delivery mechanism for fitting four threshold sessions into a base of 11-14 runs and roughly 180 km a week; the easy volume underneath is the active ingredient and is what gets stripped out when the method is repackaged for amateurs. The one controlled acute comparison found splitting a session produced LOWER within-session lactate, HR and RPE - i.e. doubles are the lower-stress option per unit of work, not the higher-yield one - and no chronic trial compares doubles with equivalent singles. Treat 'that felt too easy' as a successful session, not as evidence the pace is wrong.
- [_convention_] Cap a controlled-threshold repetition's heart rate 6 bpm under the athlete's LTHR, and expect it to settle 4 bpm under that ceiling on a long repetition (7.5 min or more) and 2 bpm under on a medium or short one. Write no controlled-threshold session for an athlete without an LTHR.
  - Round numbers chosen to keep a repetition sub-threshold given the error of the LTHR estimate itself; no study sets them. Heart rate stays a post-hoc check, as the pace rule below requires: pace is the execution target, and a session is read from minute 3 of each repetition, after HR kinetics, against the ceiling it prescribed. A %HRmax zone floor is not a threshold, so no ceiling is derived from one. Kept in `CONTROLLED_THRESHOLD.heartRate` (`src/application/coaching/controlledThreshold.ts`).
- [_convention_] Guide short controlled-threshold repetitions in a 5 s/km band: from the athlete's threshold pace rounded down to the next multiple of 5 s, to 5 s/km slower.
  - Matches the one-VDOT-point granularity (about 5 s/km) the pace rules already use; on short repetitions heart rate says nothing, so the band is the whole guide. Kept as `CONTROLLED_THRESHOLD.shortGuideBandSecPerKm`.

## Block shape and the cutback week

- [_convention_] Shape the block as three progressive weeks plus one cutback placed last, at 80-85% of the block's peak. Treat the position as movable: if week 3 feels poor, swap the cutback into week 3 and make week 4 the build.
  - 3:1 is near-universal coaching practice with cultural rather than physiological origins; no experiment establishes week 4 as the correct position, and deload intervals in other sports vary widely (6.4 +/- 1.7 days every 5.6 +/- 2.3 weeks in Rogerson et al. 2024, a survey of 246 strength athletes - cited here only to show the spread). Placing it last also means the block ends at a low-fatigue point, so the next regeneration reads a recovered athlete.
- [_convention_] Set cutback depth at 15-20% below the block's peak week, not 40-60%.
  - The 40-60% figure is Bosquet's finding for a two-week PRE-RACE taper of TOTAL volume in competitive athletes, and separately a strength-sport deload convention. Neither is a mid-block running cutback. Running practice sits at 10-25%, with one widely circulated worked step-cycle cutting only 6%. Applying 40-60% to a 55 km peak gives a 22-33 km week - which is the failure mode this system already produced once.
- [_convention_] In the cutback week keep five runs and keep every prescribed pace unchanged; cut total volume 15-20% and cut quality volume by roughly 40-45%.
  - Reasoned by analogy from taper research (volume down, intensity and frequency held) plus running practice that a mid-block cutback also exists to shed fatigue, of which quality sessions are the dominant source. No study tests this composition for a mid-block cutback in a recreational runner, and the one deload survey cited above reports the opposite practice (intensity reduced, frequency held).
- [_convention_] Declare a reduced week in the structured `week.reducedVolume` field, never by writing 'cutback' into prose, and make the exemption earnable rather than assertable: the week must be at least 10% below the block's running peak, the exemption fires at most once per block, and the ceiling still binds.
  - Validator engineering, not a training claim. The DELIBERATELY_LIGHT regex this replaced keyed on free prose written by the generator being validated, so a generator that wrote the keyword on every week could walk down indefinitely. The field is on the draft only, not on the persisted TrainingWeek, so a later weekly review reads the reduction out of the focus text rather than out of the plan - worth fixing if adaptation ever needs to reason about it. Note also that frequency.ts still gates reduced run counts on a prose regex; that one is weaker rather than broken, because a short week is a fact the validator can see for itself.
- [**contested**] Give each block one named physiological emphasis, store it in the block JSON, and refuse a block whose emphasis matches the previous block's for three blocks running.
  - Issurin's block periodization depends on SEQUENCING concentrated blocks so residual effects from one are exploited by the next, and sequencing is exactly what monthly regeneration toward the present destroys. Kiely (2012) argues the sequencing assumptions are tradition-driven rather than evidence-led, and the endurance block-periodization meta-analysis does not establish general superiority. Filipas 2022 (60 well-trained men, 16 weeks, switch at 8 weeks) found pyramidal-then-polarized best on a 5 km trial (~1.5%); there is no evidence for any particular switch point in 4-week rolling blocks.

## Where the numbers have been

- [consensus] Send each key metric's recent history alongside its current value, and state how far current sits below its own best inside the window. A single number cannot tell a ceiling from a figure still climbing back.
  - This is the same error as anchoring on a 28-day mean over an absence, one level up. For this athlete on 2026-09-23 the context said threshold 263 s/km, VO2max 54.6, CTL 44.9 and a 42:50 prediction, with no indication that all four were well off marks set months earlier: 252 s/km on 4 April, 57.1 on 29 April, CTL 68.1 and a 40:40 prediction on 11 May. Reading the current values alone produced the conclusion that a 40:00 goal needed volume the athlete had never run - when he had held 50-60 km a week for seventeen consecutive weeks that spring, and the weeklySeries showing it was already in the context. The failure was not missing data; it was a shape nobody was asked to look at.
  - Computed in `src/domain/training/trajectories.ts` from `threshold_history.json`, `vo2max_history.json`, `race_predictions.json` and the same CTL series the context already reports, using the athlete's own HR bounds so the CTL figure agrees with `load.ctl` rather than offering a second opinion.
- [_convention_] Keep the weeks with no running in the CTL series rather than averaging over them.
  - An interruption is the single most legible thing in this athlete's data and the one an average destroys. Four in ten months: 10 days from 2025-12-13, 23 from 2025-12-24, 9 from 2026-06-28, 15 from 2026-08-20. Presented as weeks of zero kilometres they are obvious; folded into a mean they look like reduced capacity.
- [_convention_] Send the prediction series as a pace, never as a finish time.
  - The rules already forbid the planner narrating a finish time it was not given, and the underlying series is per-kilometre anyway. Sending seconds-for-the-distance would invite exactly the arithmetic the rule exists to stop.
- [**evidenced**] Include bests set in ordinary training runs, not only official races.
  - Prior demonstrated performance is evidence of capacity whatever its setting. This athlete's fastest 10 km is 44:10, set on 2026-04-07 in a session logged as a tempo run; the official personal-best list has no 10K at all, so a planner reading it alone would conclude he had never run the distance.

## Where the block sits in the season

- [**contested**] Compute the macrocycle's phases from the season start to the target race and tell the planner which phase each week of the block falls in. Proportions: 45% base, 35% build, the remainder peak, with the final week to the race its own phase.
  - This is the missing half of the rolling-block design. Issurin's block periodization depends on SEQUENCING, and monthly regeneration toward the present destroys sequencing unless the block is told where it sits - which is why every block came out looking the same. The evidence for sequencing mattering at all is weak (Kiely 2012 calls the assumptions tradition-driven; Filipas 2022 found pyramidal-then-polarized worth about 1.5% on a 5 km trial over 16 weeks), so sell this as coherence and attribution rather than as performance. The 45/35 split is convention: it lands close to the structure the vendored guide gives for a 10-12 week 5K/10K plan (base-lite 2-4 weeks, build 4-6, specific/peak 2-3, taper 1-2), which was the only place that text said what each phase is FOR. That table was the guide's second load-bearing finding; recording it here is what made deleting the guide safe.
  - Computed in `macrocyclePhases` in `src/domain/coaching/macrocycle.ts`, which the plan page and the coaching prompt now share. It previously lived in `src/components/coaching/presentation.ts` and was drawn on a screen without ever reaching the planner.
- [_convention_] Keep faster-than-threshold work out of base entirely, introduce it in build, and make it race-specific in peak.
  - No trial sets these boundaries. The argument for this athlete is concrete rather than general: his 2026-09-05 5K opened at 3:56/km - faster than his 10K goal pace - and faded to 4:15/km, +19 s/km over five kilometres. The limiter is durability at pace, not top-end speed, so VO2max work in base would sharpen the end of the range that is already adequate.

## Wearable metrics and in-block feedback

- [consensus] Do not gate validateDraftVolume() on ACWR, TSB or Garmin load status. Display them; let them at most trim a plan within the already-legal band, never expand it and never veto it.
  - The ACWR critique is solid: Lolli et al. (BJSM 2019) showed the conventional ratio is mathematically coupled (spurious r about 0.52 in simulated data), Impellizzeri et al. (IJSPP 2020) catalogue the conceptual failures and formally sought retraction of the sweet-spot figure, and Impellizzeri et al. (Sports Med 2021) reproduced the injury association after replacing chronic load with random numbers (original OR 2.45 [1.28-4.71]; fixed-CL 1.95 [1.18-3.52]; random-CL range 1.16-2.07). The Garmin-RUNSAFE cohort found a NEGATIVE dose-response for ACWR. But extending the critique to TSB and Garmin's load-status classifier is a design choice, not a published finding about those metrics - say so. For this athlete the chronic denominator is travel-deflated, so any normal 45-55 km week will read elevated and it means nothing about his tissue.
- [consensus] Treat TSB, CTL and ATL as bookkeeping of what was done, not a forecast. Permit them to raise a contradiction flag (deeply negative TSB in a week the plan calls 'recovery') and nothing else. Never set a week's volume to hit a TSB number, and never use a +5/+25 TSB window to add volume back into a taper.
  - The window reads form below +5 TSB as under-rested and above +25 as over-rested. It is a practitioner heuristic from the cycling-power tradition, not a validated threshold, and giving it authority to override a volume prescription inverts the evidence hierarchy.
- [consensus] Make pace the execution target for every session with a defined pace. Use heart rate only as an easy-day ceiling, as a one-off LTHR calibration, and as a post-hoc drift check - never as an in-session target, and never on any repetition of about 90 seconds or less.
  - HR and VO2 kinetics lag step changes in work rate, so on short reps the HR reached is unrepresentative. Carmona/Billat (IJERPH 2022) found %HRmax sat at 88-91% while %VO2max fell 81->74% during a real marathon, but that is a multi-hour effort with thermal and glycogen mechanisms that do not apply to a 24-minute threshold session - it supports HR/metabolic decoupling over long efforts, not the general claim. The practical case stands on its own: pace directly measures the prescribed variable.
- [_convention_] Judge drift per session type rather than per intensity band: a threshold repetition prescribed at 250 s/km has left the band, and classifying it as interval work reports the wrong problem. validateDraftIntensity therefore keys the baseline on the workout's own type and checks drift before the caps.
  - Validator engineering. The tolerances (3 s/km for tempo and threshold, 4 s/km for VO2max and faster) come from the pace-holding rule above, not from a separate source.
- [consensus] Hold prescribed pace and let HR climb through a session rather than slowing to defend an HR number. Derive the acceptable drift from this athlete's own first three threshold sessions rather than assuming a figure.
  - Cardiovascular drift is well established over prolonged moderate work; the specific bpm allowances circulating in this dossier (5 bpm, 5-8 bpm) had no source and in one case were narrower than the stated measurement error of the LTHR estimate they were compared against.
- [consensus] Write a scheduled decision point into the block: at the end of week 3, record a 1-5 feel score and a symptom check, and record which branch was taken. If any session produces pain that alters gait, or soreness persisting beyond 24 hours, repeat the previous week's volume rather than ramping, and never ramp twice through such a signal. If a hold does not resolve it, stop the block and refer to a clinician - a plateau is not an exit.
  - Individual response is more informative than a schedule, given that two randomised tests of graded progression were null. Aubry et al. 2014 supplies the endurance-specific reason to distinguish 'acutely fatigued' from 'functionally overreached': the overload-then-reduce sequence produced greater gains, but NOT in athletes who had tipped into functional overreaching (and 70% of those athletes fell ill). The 24-hour threshold itself is convention - no validated scale was located.
- [consensus] `athlete.hrZoneBoundsBpm` is verified against Garmin's own settings and holds zone FLOORS, not ceilings.
  - Garmin is configured on %LTHR at 65 / 80 / 89 / 95 / 100, and with lthrBpm 185 that gives exactly the stored `[120, 148, 165, 176, 185]`. An earlier reading of this file claimed the value was a fallback artefact and that the app and the watch disagreed; both claims were wrong. The stored numbers coincide with the LTHR fallback in `scripts/garmin/athlete.py` because Garmin itself computes them that way, and the different floors in a September activity file are the zones that were in effect when that activity was recorded - Garmin's LT detection moved 172 -> 187 -> 185 inside a fortnight and the floors follow it.
  - Read as floors, the zones are Z1 120-147, Z2 Easy 148-164, Z3 Aerobic 165-175, Z4 Threshold 176-184, Z5 185+. This athlete's easy runs at 150-157 bpm sit inside Z2, so heart rate is usable as an easy-day ceiling and the earlier decision to leave HR out of prescriptions rested on misreading the array as ceilings.
- [_convention_] Compare an easy run with the recent normal by grade-adjusted pace when both runs carry one, and by raw pace otherwise. A run climbing 15 m/km or more (150 m in 10 km) is never compared by raw pace, so without a grade-adjusted pace it is left out of the fatigue flag, both as the run judged and from the normal.
  - Walking the steep climbs to stay in Z2 slows raw pace far more than it eases the effort, so matched by raw pace a hilly run is set beside slower, easier flat runs: it can raise a false fatigue flag or hide a real one. In this athlete's 304 runs carrying both, raw pace read about 0.5 s/km slower than Minetti's grade-adjusted pace per m/km climbed: under 3 s/km below 10 m/km, about 8 s/km at 15 m/km, and 17 s/km on the 2026-10-25 long run (19.8 m/km, climbs walked). Below 15 m/km that error stays inside the 10 s/km the comparison already treats as similar pace; past it the error fills that window. Only 1 of his 176 Base runs climbed that much, so leaving such runs out costs the normal almost nothing. A round cut read from one athlete's runs, not a measured limit; the 5 m/km from which a run's page shows grade-adjusted pace answers a different question, where it first differs visibly. Kept as `CONTROLLED_THRESHOLD.fatigue.hillyClimbMPerKm`.

- [**evidenced**] Capture prior injury history as a field and surface it in the plan rationale.
  - Prior injury is the most consistently replicated predictor of running-related injury, more so than any load metric - which is also why calling the session-spike finding 'the strongest measured association in the running-injury literature' was wrong. Captured as `athlete.injuryHistory` = `{ reviewedOn, injuries[] }`. The review date is the point: for a predictor this strong, an empty list is worth nothing unless it means somebody asked and the answer was none. Reviewed 2026-09-22 for this athlete: no injuries, so the strongest single risk factor is currently absent and the guardrails have less to do than they would otherwise. `sync.py` merges into athlete.json rather than replacing it, so the field survives a Garmin sync.

- [_convention_] Let an athlete-written note or tag override the inference the session's numbers invite, for circumstance only - never for prescribed pace.
  - Session RPE is a validated internal-load measure, but it aggregates everything the athlete brought to the session, not just its physiological demand. Illness, heat, sleep debt and travel all raise it at unchanged fitness, so an unexplained RPE rise is only evidence of fitness change once those are excluded - which is exactly what the note excludes. Observed here on 2026-09-23: RPE rose 4 to 6 against 09-17 at identical repetition pace and lower heart rate, which reads as declining fitness until the athlete's own "sick" tag explains it. Notes live in Firestore activityNotes, written from the RunOmics UI only; the athlete does not use Garmin Connect's comment field, and the importer leaves the activity JSON's comment empty.

- [**evidenced**] Ask the athlete each morning, and read the answers before the load numbers.
  - Saw, Main and Gastin's 2016 systematic review (Br J Sports Med, 56 studies) found subjective self-reports - mood, perceived stress, sleep, soreness - responded to acute and chronic training load more sensitively and more consistently than commonly used objective markers such as heart rate and blood markers. That is the substitute for HRV and sleep tracking here, which both athletes cannot supply: neither sleeps with the watch on. The four 1-5 items follow McLean et al.'s 2010 wellness questionnaire, every item scored with 5 as the best; the niggle (site, side, pain 0-10, whether it changed the run) and the ill and travel flags are this app's own. The readiness card's thresholds (ill, pain 4 or more, pain 7 or more, sleep 2 or less with soreness 2 or less) are [_convention_]: 4-6 and 7-10 are the numeric pain scale's usual moderate and severe bands, not a validated running cut-off. Check-ins live in Firestore wellness, one document per local date, and the nightly sync backs them up to wellness.json.
- [consensus] Carry the reason for each stop (athlete.trainingBreaks) and branch on it: travel, heat or personal needs no return reduction; illness, injury and pregnancy a gradual return the app does not quantify. See the return-to-training entry above.
- [consensus] After an injury, illness or pregnancy break, return by time on a run/walk progression guided by effort, with no quality work and no strides, until the athlete runs about 30 minutes continuously without symptoms. The clinician, not the app, decides when running may progress.
  - Goom, Donnelly and Brockwell's 2019 postnatal return-to-running guidelines, the UK consensus behind most postnatal advice, return through a graded walk/run progression after a pelvic-health assessment, and name the symptoms that stop it and call for referral: leaking or urgency, heaviness, pressure or dragging in the pelvis, and pelvic or lower-back pain. The talk test tracks exercise below the ventilatory threshold well enough to guide easy running (Persinger et al. 2004; Foster et al. 2008), which matters here because a Forerunner 55 has no lactate threshold and wrist heart rate early in a return is unreliable. Walk breaks carry no pace target, since a target makes the watch alert on every walk and an untargeted step adds no running volume to the week. The 30-minute continuous run as the end of the return is [_convention_], the end point of common run/walk programmes such as Couch to 5K, not a tested threshold.
  - `context.returnToRunning` computes the state, so the prompt and the validators branch on a fact rather than the model's reading: a stop of four weeks or more for injury, illness or pregnancy, until one run since holds 30 minutes across consecutive running splits. It reads split types only, with no walking-speed cutoff, since a returning athlete's jog can be as slow as a brisk walk; a free run's auto-laps cannot show a walk inside them. While it is present the volume floor is off: the walks carry no distance and the recorded kilometres include them.

## Race, regeneration and what the app may claim

- [consensus] Take race_date as a generator input. Regenerate the rolling block on the Monday of the cutback week using data through the peak week, and if the race falls within about 3 weeks of the new block's start, generate a taper block instead of another build.
  - A rolling generator with no race date will never taper, because every block looks like the middle of training. Regenerating at the fatigue trough means the next block's anchors are read from a recovered athlete. For this athlete the 10K is 2026-11-29: this block (to 2026-10-19) is a build, the next is the last full build, and the one after it contains the race and must be the taper block. The phase rule: taper at 14 days or fewer before the race, race week at 7 or fewer.
- [consensus] For the 10K itself: taper 7-10 days, cutting training-day volume roughly 30-50% while holding pace and keeping five running days. Count race-day distance plus warm-up and cool-down as a separate line item, not as part of the cut. Place the last substantial threshold session outside the final 72 hours and keep one short reduced session 3-4 days out.
  - Bosquet et al. 2007 (27 studies) is well-evidenced for the shape - cut volume, hold intensity and frequency - but its 41-60% figure is a mixed-sport pooled result for a two-week pre-competition taper. The runner-specific survey evidence is shallower (elite British long-distance runners reduced continuous running volume to about 71% over a 6-day taper), so 30-50% is the defensible band. The 7-10 day duration for a 10K is practitioner practice and runs against the duration subgroups of both meta-analyses; the volume-reduction finding, not the duration finding, is the part to obey.
- [_convention_] Do not introduce goal-10K-pace work as a substitute for interval work until the final 2-3 weeks before the race, and never prescribe a continuous run at 10K pace as a 'threshold' session.
  - Threshold and 10K pace are different intensities: at 263 s/km threshold, 10K pace is about 256 s/km. Note that critical speed sits FASTER than 10K pace for a trained runner (roughly between 5K and 10K pace), so 10K pace is not above the maximal metabolic steady state - the reason to prefer intervals at that pace mid-block is recovery cost in a two-quality-day week, not a steady-state boundary. Whether critical speed or MLSS better represents the maximal metabolic steady state is a live dispute with published argument on both sides.
- [_convention_] After a tune-up race, prescribe one easy day per 3 km raced before the next quality session, and do not re-derive threshold pace or goal pace from an untapered result without noting that it understates fitness by somewhere between 0 and 3%.
  - Daniels' published recovery rule (1 easy day per 3 km raced), which is far less conservative than the unsourced 'one day per mile'. The taper-gain figure of about 3% (range 0.5-6.0%) is a group mean measured from a pre-taper test usually taken at the end of a heavy block, so it is an upper bound on the correction, not a correction factor. Do not rank a clean race result below a threshold estimate - races are the criterion measure.
- [_convention_] Persist every metric consulted and every decision taken, including metrics deliberately ignored, in the block's audit record.
  - System-design convention. The field reversed itself on ACWR between 2016 and 2021 and the 2025 runner cohort reversed the sign of the association. A planner that logs its inputs can be falsified against this athlete's own history across successive blocks; one that does not can only be argued with.
- [**evidenced**] Say in the app's own copy that the block is organised to stay inside what the athlete has recently held and to avoid obvious spikes. Do not say or imply that the guardrails prevent injury.
  - Buist 2008, Bredeweg GRONORUN 2 and Ramskov et al. (Run Clever, JOSPT 2018, 447 recreational runners randomised to volume- versus intensity-progression, 80 injuries, no difference) are three null randomised results; Damsted 2018 found very limited evidence overall. Note also that the one prevention modality with meta-analytic RCT support is strength training, which this rule set does not otherwise cover.

## Claims to refuse

Each of these is repeated confidently in running writing and does not survive
contact with the evidence. A plan must not follow them.

- **The 10% rule: never increase weekly mileage by more than 10%.**
  - Instead: It has no experimental origin and failed its one direct randomised test (Buist 2008: 20.8% vs 20.3% injury incidence in 532 novices). Keep the 1.12 + 2 km ramp as a smoothing convention for predictable blocks and say so in the plan copy. The measured signals are elsewhere: Nielsen's >30% over a TWO-WEEK window, and the single-session spike against the 30-day longest run.
- **The 28-day rolling average is the athlete's current fitness, so the plan should start near it.**
  - Instead: A mean over a window containing an absence conflates 'did not run' with 'cannot run'. Use max(recent7DaysKm, recentWeeklyAverageKm) for the floor and the demonstrated 4-week average for the ceiling. Report 23.94 km as travel context and give it zero weight. This exact error produced the 22 km prescription.
- **A month of reduced training means significant detraining, so rebuild from a fraction of normal volume.**
  - Instead: Every detraining figure comes from complete CESSATION, and this athlete ran 45 km last week. No return-to-run protocol applies. Assume the 4:23/km threshold is intact and re-measure rather than re-derive - but put the caution on the long run, where tolerance genuinely did regress, not on weekly volume.
- **Keep ACWR in the 0.8-1.3 sweet spot and the athlete is safe.**
  - Instead: The ratio is mathematically coupled, its chronic term reproduces the injury association when replaced by random numbers, and the 5,205-runner running cohort found a NEGATIVE dose-response. A low chronic load also inflates it mechanically. Display it; never gate on it; and never read a low ratio as headroom - that is how a depressed denominator turns into a spike.
- **A deload or cutback week should cut training by 40-60%.**
  - Instead: That figure is Bosquet's two-week PRE-RACE taper of total volume in competitive athletes, plus a strength-sport deload convention. Neither is a mid-block running cutback. Cut 15-20% off the block's peak and hold pace and frequency. Reserve 30-50% for an actual taper, triggered by a race date.
- **Cite Gabbett: high chronic load is protective, so anchor on the athlete's demonstrated volume rather than the depressed mean.**
  - Instead: The anchor is right; Gabbett is the wrong authority for it. His chronic workload IS the rolling 4-week average this rule set discards, so on his own framework this athlete's chronic load is low and a 55 km week is the spike he warns about. His paper is also a narrative review of elite team-sport athletes. Anchor on 45.0 km because the athlete completed it uneventfully and no layoff protocol applies.
- **The long run should be 20-30% of weekly volume.**
  - Instead: Pure convention with no study behind a percentage threshold. Keep 30% as a loose upper sanity bound and let the 30-day longest-run reference bind whenever it is tighter - that is the one with a hazard ratio.
- **Never increase volume and intensity in the same week.**
  - Instead: Sensible-sounding and untested; the Run Clever RCT (447 recreational runners) found no injury-risk difference between progressing volume and progressing intensity. Implement the useful version: hold pace fixed across the block and change only one of {weekly km, session count, quality volume} per week, for attribution rather than protection.
- **80/20 is a law - and two quality sessions out of five runs violates it.**
  - Instead: 80/20 is descriptive of elites training 10-14 times a week, and Seiler's own session-goal and time-in-zone numbers differ within the same dataset. Two of five is 40% by session count and is not a violation. Count by time or distance, state the basis in the JSON, and treat 80% easy as a conservative ceiling on hard volume rather than a law.
- **The Norwegian method means double-threshold days, so lean in by adding a second Tuesday session.**
  - Instead: The double day is how an athlete running 180 km across 11-14 sessions fits four threshold sessions into a week; the easy volume underneath is the active ingredient. Take the intensity cap, leave the delivery mechanism. And treat 'that felt too easy' as a successful session - the documented amateur failure is not injury on day one, it is pace drift turning a controlled block into three threshold sessions a week.
- **Zone 2 is the zone, and everyone means the same thing by it.**
  - Instead: Seiler's three-zone Z2 is the between-thresholds band polarized training avoids; Garmin's five-zone Z2 is easy running. Never write a bare zone number into the plan - write the pace and the physiological name.
- **HRmax = 220 - age, or MAF's 180 - age, gives usable heart-rate zones.**
  - Instead: The Fox formula carries a standard error of 10-12 bpm, which shifts every %HRmax boundary by more than the width of the threshold band; no peer-reviewed validation of 180-age against measured VT1 was located. Derive LTHR from a 30-minute field test and rebase Garmin's zones on %LTHR, or use pace and skip HR zones entirely.
- **Take one easy day per mile raced.**
  - Instead: No experimental basis, and it costs a recreational runner more than a full training week after a 10K. Use Daniels' one easy day per 3 km raced: 2 after a 5K, 3-4 after a 10K.
- **Recalculate training paces whenever a session feels strong or Garmin's VO2max moves.**
  - Instead: Freeze the pace table for the block. Re-derive only at regeneration, cap the change at one VDOT point (about 5 s/km), and require a race or a completed benchmark session as the trigger. A Garmin VO2max bump on a cool day is not evidence.
- **A graded, well-formed plan prevents running injuries.**
  - Instead: Three null randomised trials (Buist, GRONORUN 2, Run Clever) and a systematic review finding very limited evidence say otherwise. Say the block is organised and inside what the athlete has held. If injury prevention is the goal, the one modality with meta-analytic RCT support is strength training, which this rule set does not cover.

## Open questions

The evidence does not settle these. They are the athlete's calls, and a plan that
depends on one should say which way it assumed.

- Where did the 4:23/km threshold come from? If it is Garmin's auto-detected LT pace, the whole rule set's 'anchor on the measured number, distrust Garmin's estimates' architecture is circular. A 30-minute field test or a 5K-to-15K race before or early in this block would settle it, and a plus or minus 5 s/km error is larger than a full block's permitted pace adjustment.
- Is 55.0 km the right peak? It equals his best June week and is 95.9% of his best 4-week average, three weeks after a travel month. 52-53 km would be the conservative choice and would still satisfy 'build toward the high 50s' by leaving the high 50s to the next block. This is a risk-appetite call, not an evidence call.
- Should this block take a cutback at all? Its mean (50.0 km) is 87% of his best 4-week average, and the case for a cutback is weaker when little fatigue has accumulated. A straight 48/52/55/57 build is defensible; the argument for the cutback is that one clean low-fatigue re-anchor point before two more blocks toward the race is worth more than a few kilometres.
- What actually is the longest run in the last 30 days? The block's long-run ladder is built on 14 km taken from the athlete's own note. If the logged maximum is lower, every long run shifts down proportionally even though the weekly totals stay valid.
- Is there any prior injury history, and is there any strength or cross-training in the week? Neither is captured, prior injury is the best-replicated injury predictor, and strength training is the only prevention modality with meta-analytic RCT support. Both change what this block should look like.
- What happens if travel recurs inside this block? The athlete's documented disruptor has no handling rule. A 'disruption expected in week N' input would let a monthly-regenerating generator shape around it rather than discovering it afterwards as a depressed mean.
- Does he want the second quality session to be VO2max intervals or 10K-race-pace work? Ten weeks out the interval bias is defensible, but if the Thursday session consistently costs Sunday's long run, converting it to goal-pace work at roughly 256 s/km is slower, more specific and cheaper to recover from.
- Should the rebuilt long run go past 16.5 km at all for a 10K? Nothing in a 42-minute race requires more, and the remaining ambition could go into the Tuesday and Thursday sessions instead. He has said 16-18 km is within reach; whether it is worth reaching is his call.
